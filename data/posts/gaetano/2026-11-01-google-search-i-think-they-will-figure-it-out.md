# Google Search: I Think They Will Figure It Out

There are clearly concerns around Search growth for Google.

Let’s take some time to unpack what’s concerning, what management is doing, what else I think Google could try, and what happens if it doesn’t work.

### How Did We Get Here?

I am invested in GOOG and expect them to continue to be one of the largest and most relevant companies in the world for many years to come.

‘Search’ has been on my mind for the past several quarters, especially as its revenue growth has slowed. I wanted to dig deeper before deciding what to make of that with all the renewed focus on it this past earnings.

I started with how Search has changed over the years and how Google makes money from it. Understanding that gives us a better idea of what AI could change for the business.

Picture this. The year is 2019. You want to buy a new fridge, so you hop onto Google and search for “refrigerator.” Here’s a real example from a presentation Google’s Hal Varian gave that February.

You guys have eyes, but let me explain what we’re looking at here.

At the top are Shopping ads with fridge pictures, prices and retailers. Below them is a sponsored text link from Sears, followed by organic results from Home Depot and Best Buy. (Those stores haven’t paid Google for the organic placements). You can compare prices, click through to a store or keep searching until you find a model you like.

Google also had a separate Shopping tab where you could browse products across stores, although it isn’t shown in this screenshot. By 2019, Google Shopping offered product comparisons, nearby stock and even checkout with participating retailers. You could find somewhere to see the fridge in person, but you still had to read the reviews, check the measurements and decide which one to buy.

Okay great, so how does Google get paid?

Retailers supply those product details to Merchant Center, Google’s system for managing their catalogue. They can upload a file or spreadsheet, connect their online store, or let Google collect suitable data from their website. That gives Google the model, image, price and availability it needs to match Shopping ads to a search. Text Search ads can use keywords the advertiser chooses.

Say two fridge sellers want a sponsored text ad in front of you.

Shop A offers the model you searched for and sends you to a clear, relevant product page.

Shop B bids more, but its ad is a weaker match.

Shop A can still rank higher because Google considers relevance and quality alongside the bid.

Search context and competing ads also affect the auction, and the actual click price is often below the advertiser’s maximum bid.

Google controls the rules that help determine what those retailers pay. In its August 2024 ruling, the U.S. district court found that Google had changed its general search text-ad pricing mechanisms to increase prices, often by 5% to 15% at a time, without a significant shift in advertiser spending to rival search engines. That’s worth remembering when we think about the strength of this business.

With a standard Shopping campaign, the retailer pays when you click through to its website or a Google-hosted local inventory page, whether you buy or leave. Free listings can appear alongside paid ads too. In 2020, Google added free seller offers to product panels on U.S. Search, so the sponsored labels help us tell them apart.

Here’s how that works with made-up numbers. Suppose 100 people each make two fridge searches. At an average of two ads per search, that produces 400 ad impressions. If 5% are clicked, there are 20 paid clicks. At an assumed $1 per click, Google earns $20.

Across billions of searches, you can see how this becomes a large business.

Google Search & other generated $224.5 billion in 2025, about 55.7% of Alphabet’s revenue. That category includes properties such as Gmail, Maps and Google Play. In Q3 2026, it generated $63.35 billion, up about 12% YoY. Search & other is shown in blue on the left below, while the right-hand chart covers operating income for all Google Services.

Once you have a shortlist, you still need to work out which product suits you. Google tested AI-generated buying advice through its Search Generative Experience, or SGE, in 2023, rolled out AI Overviews in 2024, then made the process more conversational with AI Mode in 2025. In the bag demonstration below, the shopper adds details about their trip so Google can narrow its suggestions.

The shopper can keep asking questions instead of starting a new search each time. The product panel includes a sponsored bag near the top right, so a retailer can still pay to reach someone making a choice.

Then in May this year they previewed AI-powered Shopping ads that explain why an advertised product suits the search, alongside its photo and price.

Google had also announced checkout for eligible listings in AI Mode and Gemini in January, initially with eligible U.S. retailers.

So the takeaway is that more of the comparison and purchase can happen inside Google.

Retailers still want to reach you while you decide, and Google is giving them more ways to do that.

### So, What’s Going On? What’s So Concerning?

Here are some arguments I’ve heard recently:

“Google is dead. I just ask ChatGPT now.”

“Why click through to a website when AI gives you the answer?”

“Google will have to spend more on AI while replacing the searches that made it so profitable.”

And here’s what management is saying.

Search query growth is the strongest it has been in five years, including growth in commercial searches.

Most AI Mode queries are additional activity.

Search revenue is still growing at the 12% rate we just looked at.

Google also acknowledged that lower monetization in AI Mode contributed to the slowdown in Search revenue growth.

They also said currency was the largest driver of the slowdown from Q2 to Q3, with retail and World Cup timing also affecting the comparison.

Google is attracting more searches while still working on how much money the newer format generates.

I understand the concern because I’ve been there myself.

I very rarely search for information on Google anymore. If I want something explained, a comparison worked through or a follow-up answered, ChatGPT can save me opening several tabs and piecing the answer together.

Think about planning a weekend away. You can ask an assistant where to stay, explain that you’re travelling with children and narrow the options around your budget. If that becomes your starting point, Google has fewer chances to bring you into its search and advertising experience. The risk grows when the same conversation takes you from an idea to a hotel booking.

Google could lose the customer to another assistant, or keep the customer while dealing with AI Mode’s lower monetization.

In Pew’s March 2025 study, a traditional result was clicked on 8% of Google visits associated with an AI summary, versus 15% without one. A source inside the summary was clicked on just 1% of visits.

In a later randomized browser-extension experiment, more than 1,000 U.S. desktop users were observed for two weeks in early 2026, with AI Overviews either present or removed. The authors found 39.8% fewer organic clicks when Overviews appeared. They detected no corresponding change in sponsored-link clicks or search frequency.

For a publisher, fewer visits can mean less advertising income, fewer subscriptions and less money to fund the next useful article.

Google depends on that material too. Keeping more of the answer inside Search could weaken the supply of information that makes it useful, even while Google’s own advertising revenue grows.

Let’s go back to the fridge. Before buying, I might ask what counter-depth means, compare models and look for stores that deliver.

If an assistant helps me through all of that, it can influence which fridge I see and where I buy it.

A retailer has a reason to pay to reach me there. If that assistant belongs to someone else, some advertising dollars could follow the customer away from Google.

Semrush looked at more than 600,000 U.S. desktop search terms and found Google’s AI Overviews appearing more often during product research.

Across the industries studied, the share of comparison-type searches showing an Overview rose 71% on average between November 2025 and April 2026.

The share showing one for purchase-intent searches fell 5% on average.

Google’s AI is becoming more involved while shoppers work out what they want, before the final buying search.

Adobe found that AI-referred U.S. retail visits generated 83% more revenue per visit than non-AI traffic in September 2026. Its comparison includes email, paid search and social, rather than Google alone, but it gives retailers a reason to pay attention to customers arriving from assistants.

Google’s AI Mode usage report shows follow-up shopping questions about electronics, clothes and home products, including nearby stores and stock availability. These are conversations Google has a reason to keep, because a retailer may otherwise want to reach that customer through a competing assistant.

In Q3, paid clicks rose 8% and cost per click, Google’s average revenue per paid click, rose 4% across Search & other.

What Google earns from AI will also depend on how often ads appear and what the answers cost to serve.

AI Mode can split one request into multiple searches and generate a longer response.

Google said in Q2 that AI Mode’s serving costs were at their lowest since launch, so the question is how quickly those savings can keep up with the extra work.

Search revenue could keep growing while margins shrink if more activity moves into lower-yielding AI formats and serving costs rise faster than revenue.

If an agent eventually chooses the fridge and retailer for me, who gets paid to influence that choice?

Google has plenty to defend, and a chance to keep those decisions inside its own products.

That’s what I want to understand as we look at management’s response.

### What Does Management Want to Do?

Google wants people to keep using Search as more of their research moves into conversations with AI.

Management is connecting AI Overviews with AI Mode so I can work through a decision, expanding the ways advertisers can reach me and building checkout tools to help me act on a recommendation.

For our fridge, that means helping me explain what fits my kitchen, compare models and buy one.

If I can do that on Google, I have fewer reasons to take the task to another assistant, and advertisers can reach me as I get closer to a purchase.

I wanted to get a fresh example of how Google was already combining shopping and AI, so I searched “I want a new fridge.”

Google showed me products with a clearly labeled Sponsored Products section alongside them.

I had to scroll farther down to reach the AI Overview.

When I added “in San Francisco,” the Overview appeared near the top and recommended local appliance stores.

With a more specific place in the question, I got a different mix of results. I though that was interesting.

Those store recommendations weren’t labeled as ads.

In my first search, I could browse fridges and click a sponsored product before reaching the AI answer.

In the local search, AI helped me choose where to shop.

Google has room to help with the decision while keeping paid shopping placements in the experience.

If that makes Search useful enough to remain where I start these purchases, it has more opportunities to earn advertising revenue.

If I want to keep asking questions, I can move from the Overview into AI Mode with the context carried over.

Google’s demonstration starts with posture exercises and ends with nearby Pilates studios.

A request for information becomes a search for something to pay for.

I can also add images and files to the redesigned search box.

With permission, Personal Intelligence can use Gmail or Photos for context without requiring a subscription.

On the call last week, management said AI Mode questions were three times as long as traditional searches.

Combined with the additional activity it reports, that gives Google more information about what people want and more chances to connect them with businesses.

On October 28, Google began expanding AI Mode ads beyond the U.S. to ten more countries, including Canada, Australia and India.

It also started launching ads on follow-up questions in the U.S., with Brazil, Mexico and the U.K. planned for later in the quarter.

I might start by asking what counter-depth means and only later ask which fridge fits my kitchen. By then, Google has a better idea of the product I need.

Google reports similar click-through rates for sponsored and unpaid product-list items inside AI Mode.

Eligible Search, Shopping and Performance Max campaigns, Google’s automated campaigns across its advertising channels, use the existing auction without a separate AI budget or extra participation charge.

For a cost-per-click ad, the retailer pays when I click. Conversion-based bidding helps set bids around the sales or other outcomes it wants.

If I don’t know what to type, I can start with a photo.

Google’s Lens demonstration shows someone photographing a backpack and getting sponsored product offers, connecting the shopper with retailers before they know what the product is called.

The more Google understands about what I need, the more useful that click could be to a retailer.

Whether it pays more still depends on the sales it gets.

AI Max uses the retailer’s existing ads, website and other signals to find relevant searches, adapt the wording and select a landing page. Google announced its move out of beta in April. A retailer can reach someone describing a cramped kitchen without writing an ad and keyword for every version of that request.

In Google’s Conversational Discovery test, someone looking for an easy way to make their home smell good sees a sponsored fragrance diffuser. Its AI explanation discusses convenience and points out that refills can be expensive.

A separate test, Highlighted Answers, places a sponsored language app among recommendations.

The advertiser pays for the placement, while Google generates the explanation independently using more than the advertiser’s copy.

Google says advertisers cannot write or approve these explainers and that buying ads does not influence the unpaid AI answer.

I need to trust the advice, and the retailer needs to know whether the ad pays. AI clicks and conversions are grouped into standard campaign reports, making AI Mode’s returns harder to isolate.

Once I’m considering a purchase, Direct Offers lets participating advertisers supply a relevant promotion.

The original pilot used a rug discount. Google later expanded it with more promotion types and a way for eligible merchants to connect an offer to checkout. I get a deal, and the retailer gets a chance to win the sale while I’m deciding.

When I’m ready to buy, Universal Cart can hold products from different retailers, track prices and surface promotions.

It began rolling out across Search and Gemini in the U.S. on October 15. Depending on the merchant, I can pay through Google Pay inside the cart or transfer the items to the retailer’s site. The retailer handles the sale, order and customer support.

The Universal Commerce Protocol, or UCP, gives Google and merchants a shared way to handle purchases without building separate integrations for every assistant and shop.

Merchants still need an accurate catalogue in Merchant Center, and stock and delivery must be checked at checkout. A fridge recommendation needs to turn into something I can actually get delivered.

Easier checkout could help more ad clicks turn into purchases, making Google’s traffic more valuable to retailers.

Shopify says its Google direct checkout has no additional selling fee beyond normal payment processing, so checkout volume alone doesn’t tell us Google’s revenue. The rollout is gradual, with limitations including no in-store pickup or subscription-only products.

Longer conversations also give Google more computing work to pay for.

Their June presentation said Gemini serving costs fell 78% during 2025 through model and infrastructure improvements.

That is a Gemini-wide measure, rather than a Search profit margin though.

On the October call, management also discussed smaller versions of its newest models that would be practical to serve at scale.

Google is building ways to help throughout our fridge purchase, with opportunities to earn from advertisers along the way.

As those features roll out, I want to understand the revenue and profit from the whole journey.

I also think the foundations Google is putting in place leave room to try a few more things.

### What Else Could Google Do?

I could give an AI assistant my kitchen measurements, budget and delivery deadline, then ask it to arrange the fridge purchase. An assistant taking actions like this would be what we call an ‘agent’. Whoever runs it gets to choose which shops to consult, which offers I see and when to ask for my approval.

Apple shows how Google could provide the technology while another business keeps that customer relationship.

In January 2026, Apple and Google announced a multiyear agreement to base Apple’s next generation of AI models on Gemini models and Google’s cloud technology.

Those models are intended to power Apple Intelligence features, including a more personalized Siri.

Apple said its AI would continue to run on Apple devices and its own private cloud system.

So I could be talking to Siri with Google technology helping behind the scenes, while Apple remains the service I turn to.

Shopify supplies software retailers use to run their online stores and already lets them manage several AI sales channels in one place.

The same store can serve several assistants.

Shared commerce standards make those connections easier for Google and its rivals, so Google needs to give shoppers and retailers a reason to keep choosing it.

Google already sells tools that let assistants retrieve information from Search.

UCP lets assistants and merchants exchange order and delivery information. The Agent Payments Protocol, or AP2, records what a shopper has authorized an assistant to pay for.

The following ideas are extensions I’d explore on top of those foundations.

Suppose 200 households need an appliance within a month and are flexible about the model and delivery date. With their permission, Google could pool those requests and invite retailers to compete for the orders. A retailer could fill a quiet delivery day, clear excess stock or win several customers at once. Buyers could share in the savings, with Google charging a disclosed fee on completed orders.

Direct Offers already helps merchants show promotions, and group buying and reverse auctions have existed for years. I’d add requests from suitable shoppers who have agreed to take part, pooled before retailers bid. Merchants would see the requirements and demand, while each shopper’s maximum budget and private conversation stayed private.

If coordinating the orders created $40 of savings per appliance compared with buying individually, a hypothetical split could give the shopper $25, the merchant $10 and Google $5.

I’d test one product category in one delivery area, measuring the final delivered price after fees and returns. Shoppers would approve the offer before committing, and retailers would need enough genuine demand to bid seriously. I’d want evidence of real savings and profitable completed orders, rather than simply rerouting purchases that would have happened anyway.

I’d also like Google to help when several things have to go right together. The fridge might come from one retailer, with a separate installer and someone else removing the old appliance. I could choose each provider carefully and still end up with a fridge outside because their schedules don’t line up.

Google could briefly reserve the fridge and installation slot, then ask me to approve the whole arrangement. The providers, or a partner that covers failures, could back a specific promise such as a correctly measured fridge installed by Friday. Someone would be responsible for rebooking or replacement if it went wrong.

Stripe already offers protection for qualifying purchases made by AI assistants through its Link checkout service. Google has previewed product-compatibility checks, UCP covers returns and order updates, and AP2 describes coordinating bookings across providers. I’d take that further with a priced commitment covering the combined result, including whether the installer arrives.

From a hypothetical $8 fee, $2 might cover expected remedies, $1 the partner backing that promise and $1 operations, leaving $4 before overhead. If it worked, another assistant could use the service too, giving Google revenue when the conversation began elsewhere. Google would have to earn that fee because merchants and rival assistants could arrange the job directly.

Then think about a reviewer who measures a fridge and discovers its door needs more clearance than the listing suggests. My assistant rules it out, saving me a headache and the retailer a failed delivery. The reviewer has created value by preventing that sale.

Google already pays some publishers through licensing. I’d extend that incentive to independently verified product evidence that reduces failed installations, returns or support costs. Merchants or partners covering those failures could fund payments to reviewers, including when their work rules a product out.

To limit abuse, I’d base the payment pool on independently measured reductions in losses across groups of orders, verify the original evidence and restrict duplicate claims. The evidence would remain available even when it sends someone away from an advertiser. Known safety information would always be shown, whether or not it earns anyone a fee.

I’d expect this to improve Google’s answers more than create a large new revenue stream. Search and YouTube give Google access to a lot of expertise. Paying for useful evidence could help keep careful testing and practical demonstrations worth producing as fewer people visit the original pages.

These ideas might not even work and if they did they would take time. But my point I’m making is that Google has so many ways to innovated and be proactive. They are far from being dead in the water on Search.

Regardless, I still want to understand what happens if the new activity doesn’t replace the economics Google gives up.

### What Happens If It Doesn’t Work?

After looking at everything Google is doing, I still want to know what happens if people move more of their searches to other assistants and Google earns less from them.

Search & other generated about $250 billion over the last twelve months, so even a slowdown in growth would affect a very large part of Alphabet’s business.

We don’t know how much Search revenue might decline, or whether it will decline at all.

In Q3, it grew 12% to $63.4 billion, up from $56.6 billion a year earlier.

The growth rate has slowed, though, from 19% in Q1 and 17% in Q2.

That’s the trend we’ll be watching as Google expands ads in its AI experiences.

At the same time, the rest of Alphabet is getting bigger.

Google Cloud grew from $15.2 billion in Q3 last year to $30.7 billion this quarter. Operating income rose from $3.6 billion to $11.1 billion.

Cloud is already adding a substantial amount of revenue and profit, which gives Alphabet another source of growth if Search weakens.

Sales of TPU systems contributed to Cloud’s growth alongside its infrastructure and AI services.

YouTube advertising is growing too. Revenue rose 9% to $11.2 billion, while subscriptions, platforms and devices grew 11% to $14.3 billion. That second category includes YouTube subscriptions, Google One and Google Play.

Waymo gives Alphabet another opportunity further out, although the Other Bets segment it sits in is still losing money.

This is why I think it’s important to distinguish between Search becoming a smaller share of Alphabet and Search actually shrinking.

Cloud is growing much faster, so it can make up a larger portion of the business while Search continues to bring in more dollars.

If Search does decline, the impact will depend on how quickly that happens and how much profit Cloud and the other businesses add.

Alphabet doesn’t disclose Search’s standalone profit, so we can’t calculate an exact offset.

There’s also a potential cash-flow payoff from the investment Google is making today.

Alphabet generated $42.3 billion of operating cash flow in Q3 and spent $51.4 billion on capital expenditures.

Management expects capex to increase significantly in 2027.

Further out, if spending levels off while the businesses built on that infrastructure continue generating more cash, more of it could remain as free cash flow. A reduction in capex would add to that benefit.

Google hasn’t said when spending will peak, and the equipment it buys will continue to create depreciation expense after it is put to work.

For me, the longer-term case rests on the growth from Cloud and the other businesses becoming large enough to support Alphabet through a weaker Search market, with more cash available once the pace of investment eventually eases.

A big part of my thesis for Hyperscalers is that the market will reward them when there is more clear sight/timeline on when their capex will flatten and decline. Before it shows up in the financials.

We shall see.
