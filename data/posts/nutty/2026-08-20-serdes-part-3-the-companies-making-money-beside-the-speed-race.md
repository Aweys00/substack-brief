# SerDes Part 3: The Companies Making Money Beside the Speed Race

Industry and technology structure analysis, not investment advice · The author may hold positions in the securities mentioned

This is the final installment of the SerDes series. I recommend reading [Part 1: The Technology You Should Know Before CPO](https://nuttycld.substack.com/p/public-serdes-part-1-the-technology) and [Part 2: The Bigger Picture Behind the Copper–Optics War](https://nuttycld.substack.com/p/serdes-part-2-the-bigger-picture) first.

In Parts 1 and 2, we looked at what SerDes is and which companies are turning the technology into money. Part 3 looks around the edges of SerDes. Sometimes the more interesting stories about a technology live at its edges, not its center.

Let’s get comfortable with a bit of vocabulary first. You may remember Part 1 comparing a lane to a single highway lane. A link is a bundle of those lanes, an eight- or sixteen-lane road running in both directions: a multi-lane road laid between two chips. And when you weave these highways together into the road network crisscrossing the whole city, that network is called the fabric. The word means woven cloth, and I can only call it an intuitive and painfully exact choice of term. But these highways are not free. Every time you use one, you pay a toll. And the toll, as always, goes to the foundry.

Whenever we talk about speed, ‘power’ has to sit in the denominator. A record speed achieved on unlimited power is useless. Engineers often express this as a Figure of Merit (FoM); in plain terms, think of it as value for money, the performance you get for the price you pay. If that does not quite land, try this: in a 100Tb/s-class switch, each 1pJ/bit of energy efficiency you give up adds 100W to a single chip.

At the end of Part 2, I said SerDes speed is one of the best single measures of a company’s engineering strength. A quick recap: based on public materials, the leading group that has carried 200G-class (224G-generation) SerDes all the way into shipping products was Broadcom ($AVGO), Nvidia ($NVDA), and Marvell ($MRVL), with Credo ($CRDO) and Astera Labs ($ALAB) following behind. MediaTek ($2454.TW), Alphawave (now part of Qualcomm, $QCOM), and the EDA houses are eyeing every chance to climb.

This installment is about the side of that map. The map shows who stands where, but it does not show who collects the toll every time the generation turns, who is a partner you must hold onto, and who is a part you can swap out. So I picked four scenes that show exactly that. The actual bill for the toll you pay again on every trip down the highway, even after buying the ticket. The price one brief outage extracts from a giant training cluster. The reason the three checks Nvidia cut within a single month carry three different sets of conditions. And the company that, without even being listed, will be the first to tell you where this map goes next. By the time you finish, this map should turn from a leaderboard into a scorecard, one that says what to check for each company, and when. The grading calendar itself is attached in the appendix.
