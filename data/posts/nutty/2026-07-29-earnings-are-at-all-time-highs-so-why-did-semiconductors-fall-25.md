# Earnings Are at All-Time Highs, So Why Did Semiconductors Fall 25%?

This piece is an analysis of industry and market structure, not investment advice. The author may hold positions in the names discussed.

### June 24, Boise, Idaho

Micron ($MU), headquartered in Boise, Idaho, reported results for its fiscal third quarter ended in late May. Quarterly revenue came to $41.4 billion, four and a half times the $9.3 billion reported in the same quarter a year earlier, and the press release carried “record results” in its very title. The outlook for the next quarter was stronger still: revenue of $50 billion and earnings of $30.73 per share. That is the company’s own forecast for the coming quarter (its guidance), and it sits 20% above the record quarterly revenue it had just reported.

The market liked those numbers. The results came out after the close, so June 25 was the first session in which the market could react; Micron jumped 15.7% that day and, even though the semiconductor index had already begun to roll over, climbed back above where it had closed on June 22, the day of the index’s high. From that June 25 close to July 28, it fell 32%.

The same disconnect between results and prices repeated at the other companies that reported. ASML ($ASML) said on July 15 that revenue and margins had both come in above its own guidance, and it raised its full-year outlook; TSMC ($TSM) reported the next day that revenue and profit in New Taiwan dollars were up 36% and 77% from a year earlier, and it guided the next quarter higher. Both fell further after reporting. The last name to report inside the five weeks was Intel ($INTC), the only one of the four whose profits summed over the most recent year (its trailing earnings) add up to a loss, and its 39% drop is the deepest of the four that reported. So there is one company that fell on bad results, but Intel alone cannot explain the decline in everything else.

The semiconductor index put in its highest close of the year on June 22, and Micron’s results came two days later. The earnings reports did not start the decline. The decline started first, and record results kept arriving one after another without stopping it.

### First, what actually happened

Over the year as a whole, “semiconductors keep falling” is not true. What this piece calls the semiconductor index is the iShares Semiconductor ETF (SOXX), listed in the US, and even after five down weeks it stands 63% above where it started the year. The precise fact is that it fell 25% in the five weeks since the June 22 high, and that the 25% accumulated over 26 trading sessions.

Set the same five weeks side by side and the drops form a staircase: 0.5% for the S&P 500, 8.5% for the Nasdaq 100, 25% for semiconductors. The whole market did not break, and semiconductors did not break alone; a force passed through the market, one that pulled harder the closer it got to semiconductors. Nor was it only an American event. In Korea, SK hynix came down 47% and Samsung Electronics 38%.

This piece follows fourteen names in the AI semiconductor value chain listed on US exchanges. In compute, the chips that directly run the AI computation: NVIDIA ($NVDA), Broadcom ($AVGO) and AMD ($AMD). In memory, Micron. In foundry, TSMC and Intel. In equipment, ASML, Applied Materials ($AMAT), Lam Research ($LRCX) and KLA ($KLAC). And in test equipment, Teradyne ($TER), with the inspection and metrology specialists Onto Innovation ($ONTO), Camtek ($CAMT) and Nova ($NVMI). Six companies with revenue centered outside AI datacenters, analog and automotive names such as Texas Instruments ($TXN), serve as a comparison group later on, and alongside all of them the piece reads the financial statements of the five hyperscalers who buy these chips: Microsoft, Alphabet, Meta, Amazon and Oracle.

A share price is the product of earnings and the multiple the market attaches to each dollar of those earnings (the PER). Micron’s PER was 57 on June 22; on July 28 it was 19. And of the fourteen names, only four published new results in these five weeks: Micron, ASML, TSMC and Intel. The rest had no report scheduled at all. The earnings numbers the market knows did not change, while prices fell by 3% to 34%, so all of that decline is PER decline.

Micron, which did report, is the opposite case with the same conclusion. Its latest report doubled its trailing-year earnings, so had the price stayed where it was, the PER would have halved. Instead the price also fell 32%, and the PER went from 57 to 19. The names whose earnings stood still and the name whose earnings doubled both had the multiple on each dollar of earnings marked down.

What the market recalculated over these five weeks was how the buyers of those semiconductors would pay for the next round of capital spending. Until now, buyers like Microsoft and Alphabet have paid for chips and datacenters out of cash their operations earned. Once part of that money is replaced by money raised by issuing stock and bonds, how many years the orders keep coming is set not only by what the customer earns but also by the terms of that funding: bond-market rates and the conditions for issuing equity. And the conditions that changed that calculation were already in place, across three separate dates, in the three weeks before the high. On June 22 itself, the day of the high, there was no news at all.
