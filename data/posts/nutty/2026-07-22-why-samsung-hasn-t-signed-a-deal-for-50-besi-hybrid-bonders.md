# Why Samsung Hasn't Signed a Deal for 50 Besi Hybrid Bonders

This is an analysis of industry and technology structure, not investment advice. The author may hold positions in the securities mentioned.

On the evening of July 21, the Korean semiconductor trade publication TheElec ran an exclusive. Samsung Electronics ($005930.KS), it reported, had begun building a production line at its Pyeongtaek P5 campus for hybrid bonding, the next-generation way of stacking chips, and had selected the Dutch equipment maker Besi ($BESI.AS) as its preferred supplier, beginning procurement for roughly 50 bonders, the machines that attach one chip to another. Neither company has confirmed the report. Amsterdam bought it anyway. Besi’s shares began moving less than ten minutes after the piece went out, and a single unconfirmed report added more than two percentage points to the stock’s gain. It happened to be a day when semiconductors were rising across the board, so the close came in above 7%.

Read that far and it is an ordinary piece of good news. The trouble starts when you turn the calendar back two weeks. On July 6, another Korean outlet reported the opposite: Samsung and SK hynix ($000660.KS) were revisiting when to adopt hybrid bonding, and the earliest it would arrive was the 16-layer version of the next generation. US semiconductor indices rose more than 2% that day, while Besi fell more than 5%, and the bonder camp of ASMPT ($0522.HK) and Hanmi Semiconductor ($042700.KS) slid alongside SK hynix. These names fell even as the broader market rose, so that report looks like the main cause. It also landed in the middle of a drawdown that had taken a fifth off the June high.

Same company, same technology, same country, and the market moved hard in opposite directions on two reports two weeks apart. Adoption is being pushed back, and yet a production line is going in. Either one of the two is wrong, or the market misread at least one of them.

None of this stays a curiosity, because the thing Besi’s bonders would assemble is the next generation of HBM, the high-bandwidth memory that sits beside the GPU and is the core component of an AI data center. Over the past year SK hynix has risen sixfold and Micron ($MU) eightfold, and running alongside that rally the whole way has been a warning that the current way of stacking this memory is about to hit its limit. Who builds the machine that opens the ground beyond that limit, and who buys it, when, at what price. This negotiation is the first time the money riding on those questions has gathered into a single contract. The curiosity even comes with an expiry date. On the February earnings call Besi’s CEO said the scope of Samsung’s adoption would be clear “early in the second quarter at the earliest,” and on the April call he moved the deadline once, saying he could speak “more definitively around the middle of the year.” That middle falls two days from now, on the July 23 second-quarter call. While the market waits for the answer it is carrying Besi at nearly 28 times trailing revenue.

Both reports were right, and both were wrong. Each one caught a different face of the same fact, and the error belongs to whoever read them as a verdict of yes or no.

### Hybrid bonding

HBM is memory built by stacking DRAM chips 12 and 16 layers high. The difficulty is in how each layer joins the next. Today’s method places thousands upon thousands of tiny solder balls (microbumps), each about half the width of a human hair, between chip and chip, and fuses the layers through them. Thermal compression bonding, which presses the stack together with heat and force, is the dominant method; SK hynix uses its own variant that melts an adhesive material into the gaps (MR-MUF), though the basic structure of joining layers through solder balls is the same. Every HBM sold today stands on those balls.

The method works, but three problems grow as the layer count climbs. The first is height. Under the prevailing industry standard, HBM has to fit inside the thickness of eight sheets of paper, 775 micrometers, and the solder balls plus the adhesive filling between them consume vertical space at every layer. Fitting sixteen layers into that budget means grinding the DRAM itself down to 30 micrometers, a third of a hair. Heat is the second. The adhesive packed between the balls conducts heat poorly, so each layer effectively carries a thin blanket of insulation. That gets steadily worse for AI memory, which draws substantial power and runs correspondingly hot. The last is wiring density. Solder balls cannot be placed closer than a certain pitch, which caps how much wiring can run between one chip and the next.

Hybrid bonding solves all three at once. It removes the balls entirely and fuses the copper pads on the chip surfaces directly, atom to atom. It is closer to welding than to soldering. What changes is only the interface where layer meets layer. The electrical channels that run vertically through each chip’s body are drilled the same way in today’s method; hybrid bonding replaces the solder ball where those channels meet with direct copper-on-copper contact. With no gap the stack gets shorter, with copper touching copper the heat runs straight out through metal, and with the ball pitch gone the number of paths can go up more than tenfold. Samsung’s own measurements presented at a conference this year show stack height falling about 15% and thermal resistance improving by more than 20%.

The reason a technology this good is still not in volume memory production is cost and difficulty. Bonding copper surfaces directly requires both surfaces to be flat to within a few atoms. A polishing step that planarizes the surface at nanometer scale is required at every layer, and a single speck of dust ruins everything around it. According to industry analyses, hybrid bonding requires an assembly line with the same level of cleanliness as the front end of a wafer fab, which is unusual for chip assembly. The machines themselves are reported to run close to twice the price of existing bonders. Hybrid bonding, in short, is better, not yet required, and very expensive if you want it now.

What Besi’s bonder does inside this process is pick up each DRAM die that passed inspection, flip and carry it, align it to the copper pads on the wafer below, and place it there. The placement tolerance is 100 nanometers, a thousandth of the width of a hair. The polishing that flattens surfaces at atomic scale, the surface treatment that makes them ready to bond, and the anneal that fully fuses the copper once the stack is built are each handled by a separate tool. The bonder takes one station in the middle of that sequence: pick, align, place.

One station sounds modest, and the genuinely hard part is repeating that motion at production-line speed, all day, without a miss. Given enough time, several companies can place a die accurately once in a lab. What a line yields is set by the speed and precision of the repetition. Semiconductor lines then put one more gate in the way. However good a new machine’s catalog specifications are, it must undergo one to two years of qualification, running real products on a real line, before it can be used in volume production. This is where the machine becomes a moat. A drawing can be copied, but the time already spent qualifying on a production line cannot be fast-forwarded. And not many companies have finished that qualification in hybrid bonding.

### Samsung’s own documents disagree on the date

Take the question “when will Samsung use hybrid bonding” to the official documents in date order, and each one answers differently.

Samsung’s first-quarter results materials this year record that it began “industry-first mass production sales” of HBM4, the current product, built with the existing solder method. The January earnings call two months earlier answered at a different level of specificity. Samsung had sent customers samples incorporating hybrid bonding, and “partial commercialization is expected” starting with the following generation, HBM4E. Samsung’s official material at NVIDIA’s ($NVDA) GTC in March removed dates altogether, introducing the technology only as the method that will “enable next-generation HBM to reach 16 layers and beyond.” Then the internal timeline TheElec reported on July 21 puts full-scale production around 2030.

Not in 2026’s volume product, “partially” in the next generation around 2027 to 2028, in full around 2030. The documents are not in conflict; the adoption of this technology is a transition that runs generation by generation. Each generation carries different volume, and a different degree of conviction.

Overlay the demand-side calendar and the split gets sharper. NVIDIA’s public roadmap has its product scheduled for the second half of next year, Rubin Ultra, carrying a terabyte of memory per package. The maximum capacity the current standard allows in a single stack is 64 gigabytes at a full 16 layers, so reaching a terabyte would take 16 of those fully populated stacks on one package. The generation after that, Feynman in 2028, is announced to stack even the compute GPU dies in three dimensions and to move memory to a customer-specific design (cHBM). From the current HBM4 the base die at the bottom of the stack is already built on a logic process, with Samsung using its own foundry’s 4-nanometer node, and custom HBM is the step that redesigns that base die for a specific customer. The higher the stack goes, the more of it is logic, and the finer the joints have to be.

Which generation actually needs hybrid bonding also gets a differently shaded answer from each company. Samsung introduces the technology as what will “enable 16 layers and beyond” and has said it expects partial commercialization from HBM4E, while SK hynix says it becomes hard to avoid past 20 layers. That argument only covers the generations that stack DRAM alone. From the generation where a logic die enters the bottom of the stack, hybrid bonding is the territory on any company’s timeline. The process TSMC ($TSM) has run in logic production since 2022 is exactly this method, which makes the dispute one about when an expensive and difficult new process becomes worth paying for, rather than about whether it works.

So what was the “adoption reconsidered” report about? It was about the current method holding up longer than expected. HBM’s maximum height is set by a standards body called JEDEC, and one fact that gets little attention is that this ceiling has kept rising over the past several years. A height limit that held at 720 micrometers for five generations rose to 775 in the current one, and industry reports suggest that the limit under discussion for the 20-layer generation beyond it, HBM5, is close to 900 micrometers. Every time the ceiling rises, solder balls get room for one more layer. SK hynix duly demonstrated a 16-layer HBM4 at CES early this year using an improved version of the existing method. If the standard keeps extending how long the old method can survive, the decision to spend heavily on the new one can keep sliding.

The “reconsidered” report was about the very next generation, about how urgent the immediate 16-layer demand is. The “production line under construction” report was about preparation aimed at custom HBM and logic stacking in 2028 through 2030.

### The equipment arrives before the product

Lead times vary by machine, and according to Besi the bonder in this negotiation takes about half a year from order to delivery. Once it is on the line, installing it, running trial production, and ramping yield (the share of what you build that you can sell) takes another one to two years. That work can only be done with real product. Stacking real DRAM on a real line is what makes the causes of defects visible, so bringing machines in after volume production starts is already too late. For lines like this, equipment is often in the fab about a year and a half before production begins. These machines can also be reused across generations. The bonder used for the 16-layer qualification in 2027 can serve again in the 2028 custom HBM pilot, and again in the full line in 2030. An order of 50 units is therefore more plausibly meant to cover several product generations.

Everyone is in fact moving this way. SK hynix is reported to have ordered one hybrid bonding integrated system for a development line back in February. And the technology itself is hardly unknown ground. Sony has built the image sensors in smartphone cameras this way since 2016, China’s YMTC has long used a version that fuses two whole wafers together in NAND memory, and TSMC has run it in logic production since 2022, with the stacked cache in AMD’s gaming CPUs as the result.

HBM is the laggard here because of how much harder it is. Image sensors and NAND can bond two whole wafers face to face; HBM cannot. DRAM wafers contain defective dies, and once you are stacking, one bad layer throws away all the good ones under it. So only the dies that passed inspection can be picked out one at a time and placed onto the wafer individually, the method the industry calls D2W, and realigning each small die from scratch every time is a step harder than aligning once per wafer. Picking and placing those individual dies is precisely the job of the Besi bonder described earlier. However much harder hybrid bonding is for HBM than for other products, this machine stands in the middle of that difficulty.

The underlying reason for the delay, though, is economics. For as long as JEDEC’s rising ceiling lets the existing method carry one more generation, staying with the proven and cheaper process was the better deal.

That makes the real meaning of “production line under construction” clear. Whenever the transition comes, whether at 16 layers in 2027, custom HBM in 2028, or the full switch in 2030, Samsung wants the machines installed and the qualification work completed so it can start as soon as the transition arrives. It is buying lead time rather than announcing a product: an option on whichever adoption path materializes.

Past the groundbreaking, TheElec’s report carries a more interesting detail: the order process has stalled. Samsung has demanded structural changes to the machine, the two sides remain far apart on price, and Samsung is meanwhile weighing Korean equipment makers as alternatives. Read that far and it is ordinary price haggling. Overlay Besi’s public materials, though, and rather more than price is riding on it. In logic, Besi leads with a precision its competitors cannot yet build. The precision HBM requires, by contrast, is a level the challengers have already reached on paper, so Besi’s advantage here is not technology but the production qualification it completed before anyone else, which is to say time. An advantage bought with time disappears the moment the challengers finish qualifying. The market with the greatest growth potential happens to be memory, where Besi’s advantage rests on that head start, and a price tag of ₩6.0 billion per machine is how that advantage is currently being valued. Both Samsung’s ability to wait and Besi’s inability to wait indefinitely come out of that. Lay the reported numbers over Besi’s published results and each side’s hand shows up in some detail, and the earnings call two days from now is the first real test of that reading.
