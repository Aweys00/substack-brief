# Meta’s Muse Is No Sure Thing

Meta is basking in the glow of its new AI agent app Muse.

On Monday, Meta shares surged 11% as investors grew increasingly excited about the app’s success. Muse handles multistep tasks such as booking flights, finding plumbers, managing customer service requests, and comparison shopping. It has been the most downloaded app in Apple’s App Store for the past several days, following its launch roughly two weeks ago.

OpenClaw, an open-source AI agent that went viral at the beginning of 2026, offers similar functionality but requires more technical know-how. Muse smooths out the rough edges, making those features easier for everyday users to access.

Meta executive Nat Friedman described the goal in an online post: “build something like openclaw that we could make safe and secure and easy to use and scale to billions of people.”

Investors are starting to run the numbers on Muse’s potential mass adoption. According to Morgan Stanley, every 100 million users would add 35 cents to Meta’s estimated 2028 earnings per share or about 1% of the total and generate more than $1 billion in revenue. Scale that to billions of users and the numbers get interesting.

Morgan Stanley also says nearly $30 trillion in consumer spending could be addressable by AI agents.

I do think personal AI assistants will become a dominant way for billions of internet users to interact with AI. It’s an attractive, sticky market. The more consumers use a service, the more data the provider gains about their purchase intent and the more context it has for personalization. Once users get used to relying on a service to manage their email or personal files, they are likely to stay with it for the long term.

Despite Muse’s initial traction, its long-term success is far from assured. Yes, Meta can cross-promote it to billions of users across Facebook, Instagram, and WhatsApp. But competitors with similar distribution advantages are coming.

The biggest competitor will likely be OpenAI.

Peter Steinberger, the creator of OpenClaw, joined OpenAI in February. At the time, he posted: “I’m joining @OpenAI to bring agents to everyone.”

I expect OpenAI to introduce its own Muse-like personal digital agent soon. Like Meta, OpenAI has a large consumer user base for cross-promotion, the 1 billion people who use ChatGPT.

Smarter, more capable AI will attract and retain the most users. I used Muse over the past week, but after encountering problems in the quality and accuracy of its responses, I found it hard to choose it over ChatGPT. I suspect others will feel the same way. With Astra and other upcoming models, OpenAI will likely offer much smarter AI that works better than Muse.

And it isn’t just OpenAI. It’s only a matter of time before Google, Apple, SpaceX, and startups such as Instinct compete vigorously to build the dominant personal AI assistant. Google has billions of Gmail and Android accounts, while Apple has more than 2.5 billion active devices worldwide.

With the eventual winners in personal AI assistants still unclear, I see a more attractive way to invest in the trend, one that doesn’t depend on picking the winning app. Here’s where I would focus, what I would avoid, and why.

My preferred way to play this trend: CPU stocks.

Two weeks ago, I [wrote about the release of GPT-6 Astra and the Fourth Exponential](https://taekim.substack.com/p/gpt-6-astra-and-the-fourth-exponential). The model’s computer-use capabilities represent a major advance and will drive the FOURTH exponential wave of demand in FOUR years: chatbots → reasoning → agentic coding → agentic computer tool use.

Personal AI agents are part of this fourth wave. The key is their ability to use browsers, calendars, and other software applications to do real work for users. These actions require a ton of CPU compute and will drive tremendous demand for CPUs over the next several years. Every AI agent needs CPUs to handle a growing volume of scheduling tasks, web searches, and code execution. Eventually, all software will be adapted to include direct connections to AI agents. Those integrations will make tasks faster and improve the user experience, encouraging more tool use and requiring even more CPUs.

CPUs have been a major bullish theme for this Substack all year. We were well ahead of Wall Street in identifying the CPU shortage driven by demand for agentic AI. [See the timeline of coverage below] I still like the major CPU players, including AMD, Intel, Nvidia, and Arm.

Qualcomm is the exception. Avoid it because it has lost much of its best CPU talent to another startup.

The rise of AI agents is also negative for travel sites such as Expedia and Booking. I expect the leading AI agent providers to eat into travel aggregators’ commission profit pool as they increasingly control the customer relationship and booking process.

Most CPU stocks soared on Monday, too. AMD alone is up 30% since we pounded the table in early August, when the stock fell in after-hours trading following earnings. While it doesn’t make much sense to chase the recent rally, be prepared to buy on dips and pullbacks.

I believe the agentic AI–driven CPU theme is durable and still in its early stages.
